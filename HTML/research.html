The briefs are plain-language summaries written for this site. Each links to the organisation that publishes research in the area, where you can find the original studies and current guidance.

Systematic reviews of many trials have found that brushing with fluoride toothpaste reduces tooth decay compared with toothpaste without fluoride. The benefit is larger at higher fluoride concentrations (around 1,000 to 1,500 ppm for most people), and larger again when people brush twice a day.

What it means for you. Check the label for fluoride, brush twice a day, and spit rather than rinse. Children need a smaller amount, so follow the age guidance on the Patient Education page.

Where to read more:

Cochrane Oral Health (opens in a new tab) · Cochrane review: toothpastes of different strengths (opens in a new tab) · Cochrane review: toothpastes in children (opens in a new tab) · PubMed: review of toothpaste concentrations (opens in a new tab)

People with periodontitis are more likely to have diabetes and cardiovascular disease. For diabetes the link runs both ways: raised blood sugar worsens gum disease, and severe gum disease can make blood sugar harder to control. For heart disease, smoking and other shared risk factors make it hard to separate cause from association.

What it means for you. Treat bleeding gums as a health signal, tell your dentist about conditions such as diabetes, and keep your gums clean.

Where to read more:

NIDCR: Gum disease (opens in a new tab) · NIDCR: Diabetes and oral health (opens in a new tab) · AAP: Gum disease and other diseases (opens in a new tab) · PubMed: periodontitis and systemic disease (opens in a new tab)

The WHO guideline on sugars for adults and children recommends that free sugars make up less than 10% of total energy intake, and says a further reduction to below 5% would give additional benefits for dental health. Free sugars include sugars added to food and drink, and the sugars in honey, syrups and fruit juices.

What it means for you. Cutting the number of sugary drinks and snacks through the day does more for your teeth than any single product.

Where to read more:

WHO: Sugars and dental caries (opens in a new tab) · WHO guideline: Sugars intake (opens in a new tab) · WHO: Oral health (opens in a new tab)

Silver diamine fluoride is a liquid painted on a cavity. Clinical trials show it can arrest decay in children's teeth, and it is useful when drilling is difficult, for example in very young children. The treated area turns dark, and it needs reapplication, so the family should agree to this before treatment.

What it means for you. If your dentist suggests it, ask about the staining, how often it is applied, and what happens if decay continues.

Where to read more:

American Dental Association (opens in a new tab) · Cochrane Oral Health (opens in a new tab) · PubMed: silver diamine fluoride in children (opens in a new tab)

A Cochrane review of trials comparing powered and manual toothbrushes found that powered brushes, especially those with an oscillating-rotating head, reduced plaque and gum inflammation more than manual brushes. The difference was modest, and its importance for long-term outcomes is uncertain.

What it means for you. Use the brush you will use properly, twice a day for two minutes. An electric brush with a timer can help people who rush.

Where to read more:

Cochrane Oral Health (opens in a new tab) · PubMed: powered versus manual toothbrushing (opens in a new tab)

Dental sealants are thin coatings painted onto the chewing surface of back teeth, where grooves trap food and plaque and toothbrush bristles struggle to reach. Public health reviews find that sealants substantially reduce decay in these teeth for years after placement, and they can be checked and reapplied at routine visits.

What it means for you. Ask your dentist whether your child's permanent molars should be sealed once they have fully come through.

Where to read more:

CDC: Oral health (opens in a new tab) · CDC: Cavity facts (opens in a new tab) · PubMed: pit and fissure sealants (opens in a new tab)
