Cavities

How a cavity forms, and how to stop it

A cavity is a hole in a tooth caused by decay. Bacteria in plaque turn sugars into acid, and the acid slowly dissolves the hard surface of the tooth. Early on, this damage can be repaired by fluoride and fewer sugar attacks. Once a hole forms, it needs a filling.

What helps

  • Brush twice a day with fluoride toothpaste.
  • Keep sugary foods and drinks to mealtimes.
  • Drink water between meals, and tap water if it is fluoridated.
  • Ask your dentist about fluoride varnish and sealants.
Stage What is happening What you may notice
1. White spot Minerals leave the enamel A chalky patch, usually no pain. Can be reversed.
2. Enamel breakdown The surface collapses into a small hole A rough spot or a dark mark. Needs a filling.
3. Into the dentine Decay spreads faster in the softer layer Sensitivity to sweets, hot or cold.
4. Into the nerve The pulp becomes inflamed or infected Throbbing pain, sometimes swelling. See a dentist promptly.

Gum disease

From bleeding gums to loose teeth

Gingivitis is inflammation caused by plaque along the gumline. Gums look red and puffy and bleed when you brush. It is reversible with good cleaning. If it is not treated, it can progress to periodontitis, where the bone that holds the teeth is slowly lost.

Signs to watch for

  • Gums that bleed when you brush or floss
  • Red, swollen or tender gums
  • Persistent bad breath
  • Gums pulling away from teeth, or teeth that feel loose

Smoking, diabetes and a family history raise your risk. Do not stop brushing because gums bleed. Clean more carefully, and book a check-up.

The mouth–body link

Why oral health matters beyond your teeth

The mouth is the entrance to the body, and long-term gum inflammation puts a steady load on the immune system. Research has found links with several conditions. Most are associations, which means the two occur together more often than chance, not proof that one causes the other.

  • Diabetes. The link runs both ways. Good gum care helps blood sugar management.
  • Heart disease and stroke. People with gum disease have a higher risk, and shared causes such as smoking play a part.
  • Pregnancy. Gum disease has been associated with early birth and low birth weight.
  • Chest infections. In frail or hospitalised people, mouth bacteria can be inhaled into the lungs.

Daily habits

The two-minute brush timer

Brush each quadrant for 30 seconds: upper right, upper left, lower left, lower right. Do the outer, inner and chewing surfaces in each.

2:00remaining
Upper rightUpper leftLower leftLower right

Press start when the brush is ready.

  • Use a soft brush with a small head, and light pressure.
  • Angle the bristles about 45 degrees toward the gumline.
  • Spit out the foam, but do not rinse with water.
  • Clean between your teeth once a day with floss or interdental brushes.
  • Mouthwash is optional. It does not replace brushing.

Diet

How often you eat sugar matters most

Every time you eat or drink something sugary, plaque makes acid for about an hour. Sipping a sugary drink all morning is worse for teeth than having it once with a meal. The World Health Organization advises keeping free sugars below 10% of daily energy, and below 5% for added benefit.

  • Choose water or plain milk between meals.
  • Keep sweets, juice and fizzy drinks to mealtimes.
  • Pick snacks such as cheese, nuts and raw vegetables.
  • Read labels for sugar under other names, such as syrup and dextrose.

Children

Starting good habits early

  • Start brushing when the first tooth appears, with a rice-grain smear of fluoride toothpaste, until age 3.
  • From age 3 to 6, use a pea-sized amount, and watch to make sure it is spat out.
  • Help your child brush until they can write neatly, usually around age 7 or 8.
  • Do not put a baby to bed with a bottle of anything except water.
  • Book a first dental visit by the first birthday.
  • Ask about sealants, which protect the grooves of back teeth.

Dental emergencies

When to get help quickly

Go to emergency care now

Swelling of the face, jaw or neck, a fever with toothache, or trouble swallowing or breathing. These can signal a spreading infection.

Knocked-out adult tooth

Hold it by the crown, not the root. Rinse it gently, push it back into the socket if you can, or keep it in milk. See a dentist within 30 minutes if possible.

Severe toothache

Rinse with warm water, floss gently to remove food, and take pain relief as the label directs. Call your dentist the same day.
